*The piece below is satire. The events it riffs on are real and listed with sources at the bottom. The jokes are jokes - the allegations remain allegations, no quotes were invented, and no CEOs were reinstated in the writing of this column.*

The corporate theatre continues to operate with its usual flawless precision. Last week, the seismic shift in leadership - the departure of Jensen Huang - sent the market reeling. A ten per cent fall immediately followed, dragging the valuation down to $3.7 trillion from nearly $6 trillion in October. The narrative was set: a reckoning, a necessary purge.

Then came the earnings call. The interim CEO, Colette Kress, stepped up to deliver the figures. The numbers, delivered with the requisite blandness, were impressive: Q3 revenue hit $115.84 billion, a 103 per cent increase year on year, and earnings stood at $2.68 per share, comfortably beating the consensus estimate of $109.15 billion. The stock, however, refused to fully accept the narrative of chaos. After the results and the chairman’s pronouncement, shares edged up five per cent in after-hours trading.

The timing of the pronouncements, of course, is where the true artistry lies. Hours before the call, Huang offered a grand gesture, an invitation to return if the Board deemed it in the best interest of shareholders and America. Hours later, the chairman, Harvey Jones, stated plainly, by first name, that Jensen is not coming back. The apology timing is impeccable.

The corporate grammar of refusal is particularly noteworthy. Jones stated that Nvidia "does not intend to reinstate Jensen as CEO". This is the language of absolute finality, delivered with the same studied neutrality as the revenue figures. Meanwhile, the machinery of oversight is clearly in motion. The image of three House committees and the SEC queueing up while Kress calmly asserts that "Our ambition will not change" provides the perfect backdrop. The history, stretching back to 1993 when Huang co-founded the company, suggests a long game, a slow burn rather than a sudden conflagration.

We are left with a company that has weathered the storm, not because of a sudden pivot, but because the underlying engine remains intact. The focus remains fixed on the immediate operational reality, ignoring the preceding drama. The entire edifice of corporate crisis management seems predicated on the idea that the status quo is the only stable outcome.

It is business as usual.

## The facts behind the jokes

On Tuesday's earnings call, Nvidia's board chairman Harvey Jones said the company "does not intend to reinstate Jensen as CEO", hours after Huang's first public statement apologised for his mistakes and offered to return "if the Board of Directors decides that my return is in the best interest of our shareholders, our employees, the company, and America" (Guardian, 17 Nov). The same day Nvidia reported Q3 FY2027 revenue of $115.84bn, up 103% year on year and 20% on the previous quarter, with earnings of $2.68 per share, beating Bloomberg consensus of $109.15bn and $2.47 (Nvidia investor relations, Guardian). The shares had fallen more than 10% after the ouster, cutting the valuation to $3.7tn from nearly $6tn in October, then rose 5% in after-hours trading after the results and the refusal (Guardian). Interim CEO Colette Kress, the 13-year CFO, delivered the results - "We delivered another outstanding quarter", "Our ambition will not change", and, on running the business, "it's business as usual" (Guardian). Huang, who co-founded Nvidia in 1993 and had about 60 direct reports, agreed on Tuesday to transcribed interviews with three House committees in early December, and the SEC has opened an investigation into Nvidia's China business (Guardian, Wall Street Journal). President Trump's verdict on a return, for the scrapbook: "I would not mind that - I think he learned a big lesson" (Guardian).
