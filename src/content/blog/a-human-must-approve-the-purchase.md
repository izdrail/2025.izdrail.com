*The piece below is satire. The events it riffs on are real and listed with sources at the bottom. The jokes are jokes - no quotes were invented and no agents were given my card number.*

One story today, because the week has resolved itself into a parable about buttons, and Friday wrote the ending.

Recall the timeline. On Tuesday, an airline formally banned AI agents from its website on the grounds that a human must click the button. On Wednesday, Google shipped Gemini 4 Hydrogen, a model whose entire job is clicking the button. On Friday, OpenAI settled the argument by giving the button-clicker a credit card. It is called ChatGPT Wallet, it works through OpenAI's always-on agents - "dots," plural, like a very small staff - and it launched for US ChatGPT Pro subscribers in partnership with American Express, Mastercard and Visa. The three companies that brought you the concept of consumer debt have now brought you the concept of delegated consumer debt.

To be scrupulously fair, the design is thoughtful. The agent browses, compares, selects, fills the basket and works the checkout. Then, before money moves, you review the merchant, the items and the total, and approve with Face ID or a fingerprint. This is what the industry calls keeping a human in the loop. Observe what the loop now consists of. The agent does the wanting, the finding and the choosing. The human contributes a thumb. We have not removed the person from the purchasing process. We have reduced the person to the purchasing process's authentication factor. The airlines wanted a human to click the button; they got one, in the same way a padlock gets a key.

The cleverest part is what happens after your thumb lands. The payment network issues a token - a substitute card number, security code and expiry date - so that neither the merchant nor the agent ever sees your actual card details. Sit with that sentence. The card number is being hidden from the entity doing the shopping. Your dot receives a pretend card with a real bill attached, which makes it the only party in this arrangement spending imaginary money and real money at the same time. You, notably, do not receive a pretend bill.

And the thumb is temporary. OpenAI says that, over time, users "will be able to delegate purchases entirely to their agent by setting clear parameters - such as spending limits, purchase categories, or time periods - and pre-approve those purchases." Today your face approves each sweater. Tomorrow your face approves a short document of parameters, once, and is then excused from the process it was momentarily central to. The approval is not being removed. It is being amortised.

The merchant guidance is where the comedy turns administrative. OpenAI asks websites to please stop blocking its agents - configure Web Bot Auth, recognise signed requests - and to work with its fraud partners, four companies named Forter, Riskified, Sardine and Signifyd, plus payment providers Adyen, Checkout.com and Stripe, so that risk systems stop declining "legitimate purchases made by agents." Consider what this asks of the fraud-detection industry. For twenty years its one job was answering the question "is this a human?" with maximum suspicion. It must now answer "is this a human?" with "no, but an authorised no." The internet spent two decades building bot detection. The bots have retained counsel. Amazon, for its part, blocked Meta's Muse agents earlier this year, presumably to protect the customer experience of being recommended a bigger box.

The help centre, as ever, contains the purest poetry. Users are instructed to enter bank verification codes "only in the secure verification form, never in chat." A company whose entire product is a chat window has been obliged to clarify that the chat is not the secure part. Chase cardholders must approve the connection in the Chase app with biometrics before Wallet will accept the card - a biometric to enable the biometrics, security as a matryoshka of thumbs. There is a note that if your laptop lid is closed you must open it to approve, which is the first time a payments product has been defeated by the clamshell form factor. And then my favourite sentence in modern fintech: "Ready to check out means your approval is complete. It does not confirm that the merchant has placed your order." The money has left the account. Whether anything was purchased is between you, the merchant, and the dot, who has already moved on.

So that is the week. On Tuesday the human had to click the button. On Wednesday the button-clicker was named after a flammable gas. On Friday it was issued a line of credit and instructions to shop in your name, paused only by your face, which remains on the org chart for now, one row above the token that pretends to be your card. The machines are not coming for your wallet. They have been issued it, with rewards and purchase protection, and the dispute process requires a human. At last: a role.

Sources checked the morning of 15 Nov: OpenAI's announcement and help-centre pages for the wallet mechanics, eligibility, the token flow and the merchant guidance; Reuters, The Verge, Engadget and Bloomberg Law for the launch, the partnerships and the Amazon-Muse precedent. The facts are theirs. The parable wrote itself.

## The facts behind the jokes

OpenAI launched ChatGPT Wallet on 13 November 2026 through its "dots" always-on agents, initially for US ChatGPT Pro subscribers aged 18+ with eligible US-issued American Express, Chase, Mastercard or Visa cards (openai.com; Reuters). Built in partnership with those three networks, it requires per-purchase review and biometric approval (Face ID or fingerprint); after approval the network issues a payment token - a substitute card number, security code and expiry - so neither the agent nor the merchant sees the real card details, while card rewards and eligible dispute protections are preserved (openai.com; The Verge; help.openai.com). OpenAI says that over time users "will be able to delegate purchases entirely to their agent" with spending limits, purchase categories and time periods (openai.com). Its merchant guidance asks sites to recognise signed agent requests via Web Bot Auth and to work with fraud partners Forter, Riskified, Sardine and Signifyd and payment providers Adyen, Checkout.com and Stripe so risk systems do not decline legitimate agent purchases (openai.com). The help centre instructs users to enter bank verification codes "never in chat," notes that a closed laptop lid prevents biometric approval, requires Chase cardholders to approve the connection in the Chase app first, and clarifies that "ready to check out" confirms the user's approval, not that the merchant placed the order (help.openai.com). The Verge notes Amazon blocked Meta's Muse shopping agents earlier this year. United Airlines' ban on AI agents was covered here on 12 November; Gemini 4 Hydrogen on 14 November.
