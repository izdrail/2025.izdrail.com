*The piece below is satire. The events it riffs on are real and listed with sources at the bottom. The jokes are jokes - no quotes were invented and nobody is accused of anything beyond chemistry.*

One story today, because Google has started naming its AI models after the periodic table, and somebody in Mountain View has a sense of humour they are not telling us about.

On Wednesday, Google released Gemini 4 Hydrogen, the fast, inexpensive member of the Gemini 4 family. It joins Gemini 4 Argon, released in October to "solve your hardest problems," and Gemini 4 Carbon, released to the public on Monday after a two-week exclusive engagement with cybersecurity defenders. Read those names again. Argon is a noble gas, famous for reacting with absolutely nothing under any circumstances, which makes it an honest name for a frontier reasoning model. Carbon is the element of life, which is a reasonable name for the smart one. And the new one, the cheap fast one whose entire job is to autonomously execute multi-step tasks in production - clicking, typing, calling tools, fixing its own mistakes - is named after the element best known for escaping from every container it has ever been put in and, on one memorable occasion in 1937, ending the airship industry.

Of the 118 available elements, the agent went to the flammable one. This is like naming your self-driving car "Titanic" because it is big and full of passengers.

The timing is exquisite. On Tuesday, as this newsletter noted, an airline formally banned AI agents from clicking buttons on its website, on the grounds that a human must click the button. On Wednesday, Google shipped a model whose entire purpose is clicking the button, and named it after a lifting gas. You cannot fly with it. That is not a policy position, it is just chemistry repeating itself.

The internal org chart is where it gets beautiful. Google's new Orchestrator feature lets the models work as a team: Carbon "coordinates," while Hydrogen powers the "worker and verifier agents." So the management model is named after the element of life, and the labour model is named after the most abundant element in the universe, which is also the one most likely to leave. Every senior engineer reading this has already been in this meeting. Carbon thinks longer. Hydrogen ships. Argon, nobly, does not participate.

Orchestrator, Google says, reaches "near-Carbon task success rates" while consuming 24% less quota and finishing 19% faster. Translate: the worker model does almost the same job as the executive model for three quarters of the budget. The workers have known this about executives for years. It is nice to have it benchmarked.

Then there is the pricing, which is its own little periodic table. Hydrogen costs $1.50 per million input tokens and $7.50 per million output - flat across the whole million-token context window, a pricing structure Google describes with the word "flat" the way discount airlines describe the word "fare." It is, as VentureBeat noted, the pricier successor to Gemini 3.8 Flash: double the introductory rate, in fact. But relax. On 1 January, Flash's price rises to meet it. The cheap model did not get expensive so much as the concept of cheap is being sunsetted. Google has not lowered the price of intelligence. It has raised the price of the alternative, and called the collision "availability for everyone."

The benchmarks deserve their own footnote in the naming inquiry. Hydrogen beats Argon on the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index by a score of 51 to 50 - a photo finish, one whole point, on an index - and leads its class on something called Agents' Last Exam, which it passes with 71.6%. Agents' Last Exam. Humanity's Last Exam already exists; somebody looked at that name and decided the agents needed one too. There is also a benchmark called Terminal-Bench, on which Hydrogen scored 57% against its predecessor's 18.2%. Terminal. The industry that named its fast model Hydrogen has a benchmark called Terminal and an exam called Last. The copy writes itself; Google merely has to sign it.

My favourite number in the announcement is that Hydrogen cuts "fabricated claims by more than 5x" compared with the old Flash. Which means the previous model's relationship with the truth was so relaxed that a fivefold reduction in lying is a headline feature. Imagine buying a car advertised as "crashes 5x less than our last one." You would ask about the last one. Nobody asks about the last one. The last one is in production right now, summarising your documents.

To be fair - and the fairness is genuine - the early quotes are glowing, the speed is real, and one biotech partner says the model "reasoned like a cautious safety reviewer." Cautious. Hydrogen. Being cautious with hydrogen is exactly how the airship industry talked, right up until it didn't.

That's today: the element that leaks out of every vessel, put in charge of execution, at double the old price, passing its Last Exam with a solid B-minus. Sources checked the morning of 14 Nov: Google's announcement for the model, the prices, the Orchestrator org chart and the partner quotes; Google's Carbon and Argon posts and The Verge for the family tree and the cybersecurity-defenders debut; VentureBeat and The Marginal Token for the pricing archaeology; Artificial Analysis for the photo finish. The facts are theirs. The chemistry is everyone's.

## The facts behind the jokes

Google announced Gemini 4 Hydrogen on 12 November 2026, pitched for "everyday coding and agentic workflows," priced flat at $1.50 per million input tokens and $7.50 per million output across a 1 million-token context window (blog.google). It joins Gemini 4 Argon (released 13 October, "built to solve your hardest problems") and Gemini 4 Carbon (public 10 November after two weeks with cybersecurity defenders; The Verge reports an agent "swarm" feature; The Next Web notes it arrived at Argon's launch price but "thinks longer"). Google says Hydrogen surpasses Argon on the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index (51 vs 50, 54 at the "xhigh" setting), finishes tasks 2.5x faster than Gemini 3.8 Flash, more than triples Flash's Terminal-Bench 4.1 score (57% vs 18.2%), cuts fabricated claims by more than 5x, and scores 71.6% on "Agents' Last Exam v1.1" (blog.google; Artificial Analysis). VentureBeat's headline calls Hydrogen the "faster, smarter, pricier Flash successor"; The Marginal Token and datastudios note the $1.50/$7.50 rates are double 3.8 Flash's introductory pricing, with Flash's rates scheduled to rise to the same level on 1 January 2027 (VentureBeat; The Marginal Token; datastudios). Google's new Orchestrator in Antigravity switches between Carbon and Hydrogen in one session - Carbon coordinating, Hydrogen running worker and verifier agents - reaching near-Carbon success rates with 24% less quota and 19% faster completion (blog.google). United Airlines' ban on AI agents booking on its site was covered here on 12 November. Hydrogen is, chemically, flammable and prone to leaking; the Hindenburg burned in 1937. Google's benchmarks and partner quotes are its own marketing and should be read as such.
