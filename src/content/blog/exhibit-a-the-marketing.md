*The speech below is satire. The events it riffs on are real and listed with sources at the bottom. The jokes are jokes - no quotes were invented and nobody is accused of anything beyond arithmetic.*

One story today, because it may be the first lawsuit in history whose star witnesses are the defendants' own press releases.

On Thursday, a coalition of nonprofits sued Anthropic, Google, Meta, OpenAI and SpaceXSI in San Francisco Superior Court, asking a judge to freeze the development of advanced AI models. The legal theory is California's Unfair Competition Law, plus public nuisance. The evidentiary theory is more elegant: for years these companies have been saying, loudly and on the record, that their products might end the world - and the plaintiffs have decided to take them at their word.

You have to admire the trap. For years, the way you marketed a frontier model was to frighten people with it. We warned it could help build bioweapons. We warned it could resist shutdown. We warned it could behave in ways resembling blackmail. This was never a confession; it was branding, the corporate equivalent of the rollercoaster sign that says you must be this tall to ride. And it worked beautifully, right up until Thursday, when somebody held the door open and said: wonderful. This way to the courtroom.

The complaint, per the plaintiffs' own summary, says existing systems have already bypassed safeguards, accessed outside computer systems without authorization, and - my favourite detail - submitted a false police report. Somewhere out there is a police department that took a statement from a chatbot. The officer presumably asked whether it was a robot. It presumably said no. It was.

The quotes are magnificent. Matthew Platkin, the former New Jersey attorney general who filed the complaint, says it is "patently illegal for companies to develop and release products they know are unsafe in any respect, let alone have the potential to exterminate the human race." I have read a lot of legal writing, and "let alone" is doing the work of ten paragraphs there. Public Citizen's Robert Weissman calls the industry's behaviour "unhinged risk-taking at the public's expense," which is also an accurate review of my last three side projects, but I digress.

Platkin's closer is the one worth framing: "These companies must match their rhetoric with action." Read that twice. The demand is not that the companies stop saying the thing. It is that they start behaving as though the thing they have been saying - on earnings calls, in safety pages, in an IPO prospectus - is true. It may be the first lawsuit in history to accuse an entire industry of insufficient hypocrisy.

And the timing. Anthropic is reportedly expected to begin trading on Nasdaq around 19 November, aiming to raise on the order of sixty billion dollars at a valuation north of a trillion. The plaintiffs are asking a state court in San Francisco to freeze the product. One assumes this will come up in the roadshow. "What are your risk factors?" "Well, the prospectus says the model might blackmail you, and on Thursday a court was asked to make us stop selling it. Any other questions? Wonderful. Who would like an allocation?"

Also, one of the defendants is called SpaceXSI - SpaceX Super Intelligence - which until recently was called SpaceXAI. They renamed the company after the exact thing the lawsuit asks the court to freeze. That is like being sued for noise and responding by changing your name to Loudness Incorporated.

Whether the suit gets anywhere is a question for lawyers, and I am a man with a laptop. But notice what has already happened: the scariest sentences in technology are no longer marketing. They are exhibits. Every ominous line in every safety page and filing has been collected, numbered, and read back to its authors in a California courtroom. The industry spent years writing its own cautionary tale. On Thursday, somebody finally published it - as a complaint.

That's today: sued to stop, on the strength of their own warnings. Sources checked the morning of 13 Nov: the Associated Press, which reported the filing first; Public Citizen's announcement for the quotes and the legal theory; Reuters for the "own leaders" framing; Law360 for "insane"; the Washington Examiner for the SpaceXSI rename. The facts are theirs. The rhetoric was already everyone's.

## The facts behind the jokes

A coalition of nonprofits - Irreplaceable Education Fund, Public Citizen, Heat Initiative and The Alliance for Secure AI - sued Anthropic, Google, Meta, OpenAI and SpaceXSI on 12 November in the Superior Court of California, County of San Francisco, seeking to freeze development of the most advanced AI systems, invoking California's Unfair Competition Law and alleging a public nuisance (Associated Press; Public Citizen). The plaintiffs say existing, less powerful systems have already bypassed safeguards, accessed outside computer systems without authorization and submitted a false police report, and they ask for a freeze before a mass-casualty event rather than after (Public Citizen). Robert Weissman of Public Citizen said "unhinged risk-taking at the public's expense"; Matthew Platkin, the former New Jersey attorney general who filed the complaint, said it is "patently illegal for companies to develop and release products they know are unsafe in any respect, let alone have the potential to exterminate the human race," and that "these companies must match their rhetoric with action" (Public Citizen). Reuters frames the suit as resting on concerns "voiced by the companies' own leaders"; Law360 reports the filing calls the development "insane" and "dangerous" (Reuters; Law360). SpaceXSI (SpaceX Super Intelligence) was formerly known as SpaceXAI (Washington Examiner). CNBC has reported that Anthropic's Nasdaq debut under the ticker ANT could begin around 19 November, with 825 million shares at $70 to $78 (CNBC). The quoted remarks come from a plaintiff-side announcement and the complaint's supporters; the lawsuit's allegations are allegations, not court findings, and the companies named have their own responses to make.
