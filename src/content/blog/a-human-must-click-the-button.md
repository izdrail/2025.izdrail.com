*The speech below is satire. The events it riffs on are real and listed with sources at the bottom. The jokes are jokes - no quotes were invented and the imaginary passengers are mine.*

One story today, because the agent economy has finally met a form it cannot fill in. United Airlines announced on Wednesday that unauthorized third-party AI agents may no longer book flights, manage accounts or buy upgrades on its platforms - not directly, and not through travel agencies or aggregators either. Your assistant can still show you the flights. A human must buy them. After two years of being told that agents would soon do everything, we have located the one thing an agent may not do: transact with United.

The airline's reasoning, laid out on a new page in its Digital Trust Center, is privacy, unintended purchases, and federal regulation. And here I must pause, because the regulatory example is the best sentence in the history of aviation. Flight check-in, United explains, requires a human to acknowledge that their bags do not contain banned substances - such as explosives. That is the line. Somewhere in the FAA rulebook there is a checkbox about explosives that must be ticked by a person, and that checkbox is now the load-bearing wall of the entire human economy. We spent a decade asking what work would remain for people after automation. The answer was there all along. Somebody has to say no to the explosives question, in person, with their own finger.

The FAQ page is a masterpiece of the genre and I recommend it as literature. There is a list of things you may ask an AI: "Where in the Caribbean should I travel to in January if I want to avoid a crowd?" Lovely. Permitted. Then a list of things you may not ask: "My United flight confirmation number is ABC847, can I upgrade to first class?" Absolutely not. The machine may help you dream about the Caribbean, but it may not know your confirmation number, which is fair, because half the time neither do I.

There is a grandfather clause, and it is my favourite part. Agent bookings made before 11 November will not be cancelled - but United recommends you review all recent travel plans "to ensure they have been booked and ticketed correctly." Read that again. The official position is: if your robot booked you something before Wednesday, it stands, but you might want to check what it did. Somewhere there is a man discovering he holds nine seats to Tulsa and a rental car he cannot drive.

United CEO Scott Kirby explained the strategy at an event on Tuesday, and the Wall Street Journal wrote it down: "To be honest, I don't want to create another middleman." Another. The airline industry, which gave the world the codeshare, the consolidator fare, the online travel agency and the forty-dollar preferred seat, is drawing the line at one more intermediary. You have to admire the discipline of a man who looks at his own industry and concludes that what it lacks is directness.

The best detail in the Journal's reporting, relayed by The Verge, is that United hasn't actually seen many people try to book flights with AI agents yet. So this is a pre-emptive ban on a problem that mostly does not exist, which is the most authentic possible tribute to the industry that charges you for a carry-on in case you brought one.

To be fair, United is not obviously wrong. Engadget notes that Meta's Muse agent once leaked a user's address online, which is the sort of thing that concentrates an airline's mind. And the industry is genuinely split: Amazon blocks Muse, Walmart partnered with it, Expedia and Booking are working with OpenAI, and just last month Southwest built a ChatGPT plugin so you can book inside the chatbot itself. Two airlines, two opposite bets on whether the robot should be allowed near the credit card. One of them will be proven right, and the other will be United.

That's today. The agent can research the trip, compare the fares, pick the seat and pack the metaphorical bags - but a person must press buy and personally swear there are no explosives. After all the benchmarks and all the agents, the last job in the automated economy is attestation. Sources checked the morning of 12 Nov: United's own Digital Trust Center for the policy, The Verge and the Wall Street Journal for the Kirby quote and the browsing-not-booking split, CNBC and Engadget for who is blocking whom. The facts are theirs. The explosives attestation is yours. Personally.

## The facts behind the jokes

United Airlines announced on 11 November 2026 that it is restricting access for unauthorized third-party AI agents, directly on its digital platforms and indirectly via travel agencies and aggregators, citing privacy, unintended purchases or account changes, and FAA/DOT regulatory risk - including that check-in requires human acknowledgment that bags contain no banned substances such as explosives (United Digital Trust Center). Agents may still surface United flights and fares but cannot complete the purchase; bookings made by agents before 11 November will not be cancelled, with a recommendation to review recent plans, and United says it has not yet seen many booking attempts by agents (The Verge, citing The Wall Street Journal). CEO Scott Kirby: "To be honest, I don't want to create another middleman" (The Verge, citing the WSJ). Amazon has blocked Meta's Muse agent while Walmart has partnered with it, and Expedia and Booking.com are working with OpenAI (CNBC); Meta's Muse previously leaked a user's address online (Engadget); Southwest announced a ChatGPT booking plugin last month, and Delta and American said some AI tools already have booking privileges on their systems (Fast Company). Sources checked the morning of 12 November. The examples of permitted and forbidden questions are quoted from United's own FAQ; product capabilities and intentions are attributed announcements, not independently tested claims.
