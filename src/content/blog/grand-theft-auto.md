One story today - Saturday, 7 November 2026

Six pallets of Nvidia hardware worth $11.4 million left Oakland on a truck and simply stopped existing somewhere before Texas. The truck was dressed as a food company, which is honest in one sense: GPUs are devoured faster than food.

## Today's cope

*The elephant is fine. It has checked the warehouse twice. The GPUs are definitely still there.*

## The facts

- **The heist**: Six pallets of Nvidia hardware worth $11.4 million left Oakland on 29 August bound for Fort Worth, Texas, and vanished; more than two months later there are no arrests and no recovered cargo (Ars Technica).
- **The disguise**: The shipment was picked up as scheduled by a truck bearing the logo of a food transportation company (The Mercury News, via Ars Technica).
- **The trail**: The theft was noticed in early September when the buyers realized nothing had arrived; police traced several multi-hour stops before losing the truck in San Bernardino County, and a grand theft report was filed 13 September. The investigation is ongoing (Ars Technica).
- **The wave**: Thieves have made off with more than $100 million in similar shipments since June, using techniques like GPS location spoofing and even arranging collisions with police escort vehicles (Ars Technica, citing investigators quoted by NBC News).
- **The margins**: UC Berkeley Haas professor Olaf Groth says a chip costing about $550,000 in the US goes for $1 million or more abroad, and that bigger cargoes and more complex heists point to professionalizing crews (ABC News, via Ars Technica and Tom’s Hardware).
- **The prequel**: In 2025, police investigated burglaries of Nvidia hardware valued at more than $7.5 million around Fremont and Union City, California (Ars Technica).
- **The other crew**: In September, thieves stole two trailers marked with Nvidia and PlusAI logos, cut the locks, and found 20,000 pounds of sand in each - ballast for autonomous-trucking load tests. The trailers were recovered nearby (Wired, via Ars Technica).

## The monologue

The beautiful part is the timing. The truck arrived as scheduled, loaded as scheduled, and left as scheduled. The buyers noticed nothing until early September, when it dawned on them that the delivery had never come. For about a week, eleven million dollars of the most wanted hardware on Earth was nobody's problem, because everyone involved assumed the truck was full of salad.

Police later retraced the route: several leisurely multi-hour stops - the crew was in no hurry, since nobody was looking - and then nothing, somewhere in San Bernardino County. Grand theft report filed 13 September. Two months on: no arrests, no cargo, and the authorities will not even say what the hardware was. In the chip economy, eleven million dollars can disappear into the desert like a gamer into a new release.

And this is not a one-off; it is a supply chain. Ars reports that more than $100 million in similar shipments has been stolen since June, with crews deploying GPS spoofing and, in some cases, arranging collisions with police escort vehicles, which is less smash-and-grab and more logistics with a demolition derby division. A Berkeley professor explains the economics with admirable calm: a chip that costs $550,000 at home sells for a million or more abroad. The GPU has achieved what took the art market centuries - a portable, high-value asset that everyone agrees is worth more somewhere else.

Then there is the other crew. In September, per Wired, thieves stole two trailers proudly marked with Nvidia and PlusAI logos, cut the locks, and found twenty thousand pounds of sand in each - ballast used to simulate freight for self-driving truck tests. The trailers were recovered nearby. Somewhere out there is a heist crew with a forklift, an overseas buyer, and forty thousand pounds of extremely hot sand. It is the perfect parable of the AI boom: enormous effort, sophisticated planning, and a payload that turns out to be literally dirt, valued entirely on the promise of what it was supposed to carry.
